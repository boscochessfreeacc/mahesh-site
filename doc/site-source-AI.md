## Where rigour meets wonder.

For over a century, Crestfield has stood apart. Not by accident, and not by ambition alone — but by design. The institute was built on a conviction that remains as urgent today as it was in 1924: that the world's most consequential problems cannot be solved by minds trained in only one way of seeing. We exist to cultivate the other kind.

### A Different Kind of Institution

There is no shortage of excellent universities. What remains scarce is an institution genuinely built — structurally, not rhetorically — around the production of integrated intelligence. Most universities speak of interdisciplinarity. Crestfield enforces it. Most speak of research culture. Crestfield is constituted by it. The distinction is not one of aspiration but of architecture.

From the moment a student arrives at Harwick Gate, they are immersed in an environment designed to unsettle comfortable certainties and replace them with something more durable: the capacity to reason carefully under conditions of genuine uncertainty. That capacity, developed through years of rigorous study, laboratory engagement, and intellectual community, is what our graduates carry into the world.

They carry it into research laboratories and central banks, into government ministries and technology companies, into hospitals, courts, newsrooms, and the founding teams of enterprises that do not yet exist. They carry it, above all, into the problems that no one has yet solved — the problems that will define the coming century.

---

### Three Principles, One Purpose

**Systems Thinking** Every problem at CISH is approached as a network of interconnected forces, feedback loops, and unintended consequences. Students learn not only to address the problem in front of them, but to perceive the wider system within which that problem is embedded. This orientation prevents the parochialism that afflicts narrowly trained specialists and equips graduates to act with genuine strategic intelligence in domains they have never formally studied.

**First-Principles Reasoning** Canonical knowledge is a point of departure, not a destination. Faculty are recruited for their capacity to teach from fundamental concepts. Students are assessed on their ability to reconstruct understanding from the ground up rather than retrieve it from memory. This discipline produces graduates who remain effective even as the knowledge landscape shifts beneath them — which, in the twenty-first century, it always does.

**Long-Term Orientation** Decisions at CISH are made on multi-decade horizons. Research programmes that may not yield results for a generation receive protected funding. Strategic plans account for societal and environmental challenges in their full complexity. This orientation is not merely philosophical: it is encoded in governance structures, funding models, and the tenure system itself.

## A century of deliberate design.

Crestfield was not founded to replicate what already existed. It was founded because what existed was not working — at least not for the problems that mattered most. Understanding why requires returning to the specific failure that called the institute into being.

---

### The Problem That Created Us

In the early decades of the twentieth century, higher education exhibited a structural asymmetry that was producing visible and measurable failures. Engineering faculties excelled at technical execution but trained graduates who could not reason about the systems within which their work would operate. Classical universities preserved intellectual tradition but had grown progressively detached from the rapidly advancing landscape of applied science and technology.

The consequences were not abstract. They were visible in the design failures of major infrastructure projects, in the unanticipated social dislocations of industrial expansion, in the inability of economic institutions to model — let alone prevent — the systemic crises that punctuated the early twentieth century. The problem was not that the engineers were insufficiently clever or the scholars insufficiently learned. The problem was that no one was training people to think across the boundary between the two.

The founding consortium of CISH — an unusual alliance of industrial engineers, mathematical logicians, policy advisors, and far-sighted philanthropists — identified this gap with precision and drew an equally precise conclusion: the problem was not curricular. It was architectural. Education systems had been optimised for stability and replication. What was needed was an institution optimised for something else entirely.

---

### The Founding Charter of 1924

The charter that established the Crestfield Institute in 1924 encoded four constraints so fundamental that they have governed every subsequent institutional decision. They were written not as aspirations but as structural requirements — deliberately difficult to amend, so that they would survive the institutional pressures that inevitably accumulate over time.

**Research primacy.** The institute would be research-first, not instruction-first. Teaching would follow from the research culture, not the reverse.

**Faculty governance.** Academic governance would be protected from short-term external pressures, whether commercial, political, or philanthropic. The independence of the scholarly community was non-negotiable.

**Continuous revision.** Curriculum would be permanently revisable in response to emerging knowledge domains. No subject would be protected by tradition alone.

**Structural interdisciplinarity.** Interdisciplinary work would be incentivised at the level of resource allocation and institutional design — not merely encouraged in rhetoric and left to wither in practice.

The founders also made a fifth decision that distinguished CISH from every comparable institution of the era. They rejected scale. The institute was deliberately designed to remain small enough to sustain intellectual density — the quality of intellectual environment that emerges only when scholars from different disciplines are in genuine proximity, not merely housed in adjacent buildings.

---

### A Century in Outline

**1905–1924: Formation** The intellectual conditions that would give rise to CISH develop across the first two decades of the century. The founding consortium assembles gradually, drawn together by a shared diagnosis of what existing institutions cannot produce. The Crestfield Charter is ratified on 14 March 1924. The first cohort of thirty-two students — selected personally by the founding faculty — arrives in October of the same year.

**1924–1945: Establishment and Experiment** The institute's earliest years are devoted to developing pedagogical methods adequate to its founding ambitions. Curriculum is constructed as an interconnected system rather than a sequence of isolated subjects. Laboratory-based learning — extending beyond experimental science into systems simulation, early computational environments, and engineering design studios — becomes the defining mode of instruction. The institute weathers the interruption of the Second World War by pivoting its research capacity toward applied problems in communications, logistics, and materials science, many of which produce results with enduring peacetime applications.

**1945–1965: Consolidation** The postwar period brings both challenge and opportunity. Demand for higher education expands dramatically across the developed world, and pressure on CISH to grow in response is considerable. The institute resists, holding to its founding commitment to scale, and directs its energy instead toward deepening its research infrastructure and building the graduate programme that would, within a decade, become one of the most competitive in Europe.

**1965–2000: Research Primacy** The global acceleration of science and technology in the second half of the twentieth century confirms the founding diagnosis and strengthens the case for CISH's model. The institute formalises its transition to a research-intensive university. Departmental structures are gradually supplemented — and in several cases replaced — by problem-oriented research clusters. The first external research partnerships are established with industry, on terms that preserve the institute's academic independence. By 2000, CISH is ranked among the top twenty research universities in the world.

**2000–2025: Contemporary Architecture** The modern core curriculum integrates probability and statistical inference, computational thinking, and applied ethics as foundational requirements. Self-designed interdisciplinary programmes become a structural feature of the degree system. The institute's research output reaches record levels, with faculty publishing in the highest-impact journals across every major field. In 2022, the _Times Higher Education_ World University Rankings place CISH eighth globally — the highest position in the institute's history.

**2025–2045: The Second Century** Crestfield enters its second century with a defined strategic agenda. Four research domains — AI governance, climate systems, bio-computational interfaces, and distributed infrastructure — anchor the institute's forward planning. Moderate growth in student population is planned. A new research campus is under development two miles from the main site. Global partnerships are being deepened. The founding questions remain unanswered in their fullest form, which is precisely why the institute remains necessary.

### Academic Structure

CISH is organised into three schools, each of which operates across disciplinary boundaries and contributes to the institute's interdisciplinary research clusters.

**The School of Mathematical and Computational Sciences** encompasses mathematics, statistics, theoretical computer science, and the formal foundations of logic and information theory.

**The School of Natural and Engineering Sciences** encompasses physics, chemistry, materials science, biological sciences, and all engineering disciplines, with particular emphasis on systems integration across these fields.

**The School of Humanities, Policy, and Society** encompasses philosophy, history of science, political economy, law and governance, and the social sciences as they apply to the analysis of complex systems.

Each school maintains its own academic identity and governance structures while contributing to the institute's overarching research clusters, which draw faculty and students from all three.

---

### Governance

CISH operates through a distributed governance model that balances institutional leadership with genuine faculty authority. The Academic Senate holds authority over curriculum and academic policy. Research boards allocate funding and set the direction of major research programmes. Administrative leadership ensures operational coherence. An independent Board of Governors provides fiduciary oversight and long-term strategic guidance.

This structure is slower to change than those of purely administrative institutions. It is also considerably more resistant to the kind of drift — toward commercial imperatives, reputational pressures, or short-term thinking — that compromises intellectual integrity over time. The slowness is not a bug. It is the system working as intended.

---

### Institutional Honesty

Crestfield does not claim to be the right institution for every student. The academic environment here is intentionally demanding. The expectation that students will engage with open-ended problems, challenge their own assumptions, and work at the edge of what they understand is not aspirational language — it is a description of daily life at the institute.

Students who thrive at CISH are those who are genuinely unsettled by easy answers, who find the encounter with complexity energising rather than demoralising, and who are capable of sustained effort across the long periods when progress is not visible. We are candid about this because we believe that the best admissions decision is an informed one — for the student as much as for the institution.

## Depth, breadth, and the space between.

Every programme at CISH begins from the same conviction: that intellectual depth and disciplinary breadth are not alternatives to be traded against each other, but dimensions of a single educational ambition. Our graduates are specialists who can think beyond their specialism. That combination does not happen by accident.

---

### The Core Curriculum: A Cognitive Toolkit

Every CISH student, regardless of eventual specialisation, completes a shared foundational curriculum across their first two years. This is not a general education requirement in the conventional sense. It is a structured programme designed to establish a common cognitive framework — a set of analytical tools that will serve students through every subsequent stage of their intellectual life.

The core is built on the recognition that the most consequential problems of our time are not housed within a single discipline. They require people who can move fluently across registers — from mathematical formalism to policy argument, from experimental evidence to ethical reasoning, from computational model to human consequence. The core curriculum develops that fluency before specialisation narrows the frame.

**Year One: Foundations**

- _Mathematics as a Unifying Language_ — Full-rigour instruction in calculus, linear algebra, probability, and formal logic, taught as tools for thinking rather than as ends in themselves. Students engage with proofs, with the limits of formal systems, and with the discipline of moving from intuition to demonstration.
- _Modelling the Physical World_ — The natural sciences taught through the construction and evaluation of models. Students learn to distinguish between the map and the territory, to identify the assumptions embedded in any model, and to reason about what a model cannot tell you.
- _Writing and Rhetoric for Technical Minds_ — Intensive writing instruction integrated with technical reasoning. The conviction underlying this module is simple: an idea that cannot be communicated clearly has not yet been fully thought.
- _Epistemology and Scientific Method_ — Philosophy of knowledge and philosophy of science, with emphasis on the historical development of scientific practice, the sociology of scientific institutions, and the question of how we know what we think we know.

**Year Two: Integration**

- _Statistical Inference and Decision Under Uncertainty_ — Probability theory, statistical reasoning, and Bayesian inference, with applications across science, policy, engineering, and economics.
- _Computational Thinking_ — Algorithmic reasoning, complexity theory, and the principles of computational systems, integrated across disciplines rather than contained within computer science.
- _Ethics in Technological and Societal Context_ — Applied ethics with emphasis on the specific challenges raised by emerging technologies, large-scale systems, and the distribution of power within complex institutions. Not a survey of ethical theories, but an engagement with real problems.
- _Systems Dynamics_ — An introduction to the formal analysis of complex systems: feedback loops, non-linearity, emergent behaviour, tipping points, and the limits of prediction.
- _First Research Engagement_ — Every second-year student is assigned to an active research cluster for one term. This is not an observation exercise. Students are expected to contribute, and the best contributions find their way into the published record.

---

**Doctoral Programmes (DPhil, 3–4 years)**

CISH's doctoral programme is among the most rigorous in the world. Students are admitted to specific research clusters, supervised by leading faculty, and expected to produce original contributions to knowledge within three to four years of full-time study. The DPhil at Crestfield is an apprenticeship in the truest sense: doctoral students do not learn about research at a remove. They conduct it, from their first week.

More than 60% of current doctoral students are international. Doctoral funding is available on a competitive basis, with full studentships — covering fees and a living allowance — awarded to the most outstanding applicants regardless of nationality.

Doctoral graduates from CISH hold positions at Oxford, Cambridge, MIT, ETH Zurich, the Max Planck Institutes, the European Central Bank, DeepMind, McKinsey Global Institute, CERN, and the governments of twelve countries.

### Assessment Philosophy

Crestfield maintains a degree classification system for external compatibility, but supplements it with qualitative evaluation mechanisms that address the dimensions of intellectual development that grades alone cannot capture.

Faculty-written narrative assessments accompany formal marks at the end of each term. These address originality, quality of reasoning under uncertainty, capacity for disciplinary integration, and evidence of intellectual growth. They are shared with students directly and form part of the permanent academic record.

Research impact documentation tracks student contributions to live research programmes. Peer collaboration assessments evaluate the quality of engagement in group and interdisciplinary settings.

The underlying conviction is straightforward: assessment systems that cannot distinguish between memorisation and understanding, or between technical competence and genuine originality, are not fit for the purposes of an institution like CISH. We assess what we actually value, which requires tools more precise than a percentage score.

## Infrastructure as a strategic asset.

At most institutions, infrastructure is treated as a support function — a cost to be managed and explained to trustees in terms of maintenance obligations. At Crestfield, infrastructure is understood as a core component of research capability. The quality of the environments in which scholars work is not incidental to the quality of the work they produce. It is constitutive of it.

This conviction has driven substantial institutional investment across every decade of CISH's development. The result is a physical and digital estate that is, in the assessment of several independent reviews, without peer among universities of comparable size in Europe.

---

### The Main Campus: Harwick Gate

The Crestfield campus occupies 42 acres in Oxfordshire, anchored by the original Victorian Gothic buildings of the founding era and extended across successive decades by architecture of considerable distinction. The 2003 commission for the Brauer Research Pavilion — awarded to the firm of Gustafsen & Mäkinen following an international competition — was described by the _Architectural Review_ as "the most important addition to a British university campus in a generation." The 2019 Engineering Fabrication Centre, designed by Studio Alvarez with sustainability targets exceeding current regulatory requirements by a factor of three, received the RIBA Stirling Prize in 2020.

The campus is a five-minute walk from Crestfield village and thirty minutes by direct rail from Oxford. London Paddington is reached in 58 minutes.

---

### The Crestfield High-Performance Computing Centre

The CHPCC is one of the largest academic computing facilities in the United Kingdom. Its current configuration comprises:

- **14,000 processor cores** across heterogeneous architectures, optimised for both parallel scientific computing and large-scale data analysis
- **1,200 terabytes** of high-speed NVMe storage, with tiered archival capacity extending to 12 petabytes
- **A dedicated GPU array** comprising 640 NVIDIA H100 units, supporting machine learning research, molecular dynamics simulation, and computational fluid dynamics workloads
- **A quantum computing testbed**, established in partnership with IBM Quantum in 2023, providing access to 127-qubit processors for research in quantum algorithms and quantum-classical hybrid computation
- **Network infrastructure** delivering 400 gigabit connectivity between the CHPCC and all CISH research laboratories, with a dedicated 100 gigabit link to the Janet academic network and CERN's data grid

The centre operates continuously, managed by a team of twenty-two research computing specialists who work alongside faculty to optimise workloads, develop bespoke software infrastructure, and ensure that computational tools remain at the frontier of the technically achievable. Access is available to all CISH researchers, with priority allocation for active research projects and a tiered access model for students at each stage of their programme.

### Modular Research Laboratories

CISH's laboratory infrastructure is organised around a modular architecture that allows research clusters to reconfigure physical space as project requirements evolve. Rather than maintaining fixed facilities associated with specific departments — a model that tends to ossify research programmes around available equipment rather than genuine intellectual priorities — the institute operates a portfolio of adaptable laboratory environments that can be equipped and re-equipped continuously.

**Physical Sciences Suite** The physical sciences laboratories encompass facilities for materials characterisation, X-ray crystallography, electron microscopy, spectroscopy across the full electromagnetic spectrum, and precision measurement at scales from nanometres to kilometres. The suite includes a dedicated cryogenics facility capable of sustaining temperatures within 0.01 kelvin of absolute zero, used primarily by the institute's condensed matter physics and quantum materials research groups.

**Life Sciences Wing** The life sciences facilities include biosafety level 2 and level 3 laboratories, full cell culture and tissue engineering suites, a genomics core with short-read and long-read sequencing capabilities, cryo-electron microscopy infrastructure, and an imaging centre housing confocal, light-sheet, and super-resolution fluorescence microscopes. The wing is shared between CISH's molecular biology and computational biology research groups and is accessible to graduate students as part of their doctoral training.

**Engineering Fabrication Centre** Opened in 2019, the EFC provides CISH researchers and students with access to the full range of modern fabrication and prototyping technologies: CNC machining, multi-material additive manufacturing, laser cutting and forming, printed circuit board fabrication, and a Class 100 clean room for semiconductor research and microelectromechanical systems development. The centre is staffed by seven experienced technicians who support both research-grade and exploratory fabrication work.

### The Harwick Library and Archive

The Harwick Library is the intellectual heart of the Crestfield campus. Its holdings encompass:

- **1.4 million physical volumes**, with particular strengths in mathematics, physics, the history of science, political theory, and philosophy
- **280,000 journal titles**, accessible electronically through the library's unified discovery system
- **Special Collections**, housing one of the most significant archives of scientific correspondence in private institutional hands in Europe, including the papers of six Fellows of the Royal Society whose careers were associated with CISH, and a collection of early printed scientific texts dating to the sixteenth century
- **The CISH Institutional Archive**, a fully digitised record of the institute's governance, research output, and correspondence from 1924 to the present

The library's digital infrastructure provides seamless access to every major academic database. A team of twelve specialist librarians supports researchers across all stages of their work, from initial literature review to research data management and open access publication compliance. A dedicated data services team assists with the curation of research datasets, supporting CISH's commitment to open and reproducible science.

The reading rooms are open around the clock during term. The Founders' Room — a wood-panelled space on the library's first floor, unchanged since 1931 — is reserved for senior researchers and doctoral students by application, and has been the setting for a disproportionate number of CISH's most significant intellectual breakthroughs, if the accounts of those involved are to be credited.

The library also hosts the annual Harwick Lectures, a series that has brought figures including three sitting heads of state, four Nobel Laureates, the directors of CERN and the WHO, and a rotating cast of the most distinguished scholars of their generation to address the CISH community on questions at the frontier of knowledge.

### The Crestfield Innovation Hub

The Innovation Hub is the bridge between CISH's research activities and their application in the world beyond the campus. Housed in a purpose-built building adjacent to the main gate, the Hub provides space and structured support for researchers and students who are developing research outputs into deployable technologies, policy tools, or commercial enterprises.

The Hub operates a three-stage support model:

**Ideas Lab** — Open to all CISH students and researchers, the Ideas Lab provides hot-desk working space, access to prototyping equipment, and a weekly programme of workshops, seminars, and one-to-one consultations with practitioners from industry, government, and the investment community.

**Residency Programme** — For projects with defined scope and team, six-month residencies provide dedicated office and laboratory space, a seed grant of up to £25,000, and access to CISH's network of mentors drawn from the alumni body and the institute's industry partnership network.

**Accelerator Track** — For ventures seeking external investment or partnership, the Accelerator provides intensive support over twelve weeks, culminating in a presentation to a panel of investors, government representatives, and CISH faculty. Twenty-three companies have exited the Accelerator Track in the past five years. Their combined valuation at last independent assessment exceeded £1.2 billion.

It is important to note that participation in the Innovation Hub is never a condition of research activity at CISH, and the institute does not take equity stakes in ventures that emerge from it. The Hub is a resource, not a requirement, and the boundaries between fundamental research and commercial application are maintained with deliberate care.

### Residential and Wellbeing Facilities

CISH guarantees residential accommodation for all first-year undergraduate students and for doctoral students in their first year of study. The residential estate spans six buildings on and adjacent to the main campus, accommodating 1,800 students across a range of room types, from traditional en-suite study bedrooms to modern studio apartments for graduate researchers.

All residences are within a ten-minute walk of the main library and laboratory facilities. Broadband connectivity throughout the estate provides speeds of 1 gigabit per second to every room.

**Wellbeing Services** The institute operates a comprehensive wellbeing provision, recognising that the academic intensity of the CISH environment makes robust support not merely desirable but essential. Services include:

- Individual counselling with a team of ten qualified therapists, with same-week appointments guaranteed
- Group support programmes covering academic pressure, identity and belonging, relationships, and transition
- A peer mentoring scheme that pairs first-year students with trained second and third-year mentors from their discipline
- A physical health centre staffed by two GPs, a nurse practitioner, and a physiotherapist, open five days a week with out-of-hours telephone triage

**Sports and Recreation** The sports facilities include a full gymnasium, a 25-metre swimming pool, four squash courts, tennis and badminton courts, outdoor pitches for football, rugby, and hockey, and a boathouse on the nearby river. CISH fields competitive teams in twenty-three sports at national collegiate level. The institute has produced four Olympic athletes in the past decade.

**Dining** The main dining hall, rebuilt in 2017 to a design shortlisted for the RIBA award, seats 900 and serves three meals daily to the full campus community. A network of five cafés distributed across the estate supplements the main hall and provides speciality coffee, light meals, and working space throughout the day. The institute holds a three-star rating from the Sustainable Restaurant Association, reflecting its commitments to food provenance, environmental practice, and the wellbeing of its catering staff.

**Overline:** Admissions

## We are not looking for the student who has every answer. We are looking for the student who cannot stop asking better ones.

Admission to the Crestfield Institute is among the most competitive in British higher education. In 2024, the institute received 24,800 applications for 420 undergraduate places, and 9,200 applications for 180 taught Masters positions. These numbers are not cited to intimidate. They are cited because honesty about what CISH is and who is likely to succeed here is a fundamental part of what we consider our duty to applicants.

The selection process at Crestfield is designed to find the students most likely to flourish in a research-intensive, intellectually demanding environment. It has evolved considerably over the institute's century of operation — from a system reliant almost entirely on academic metrics to one that takes a genuinely multi-dimensional view of intellectual potential. The principle at the centre of that evolution has not changed: we are interested in long-term intellectual potential, not short-term performance optimisation.

---

### Who We Are Looking For

**We are looking for depth, not breadth.** The applicant with an extraordinary engagement with two or three subjects, who has pursued their intellectual interests beyond the curriculum and arrived at questions that their teachers could not answer, interests us more than the applicant who has accumulated a wide portfolio of achievements without particular passion for any of them. CISH is not impressed by comprehensiveness. It is impressed by genuine curiosity and the rigorous pursuit of it.

**We are looking for comfort with uncertainty.** The ability to operate effectively in conditions of genuine ambiguity — where the problem is not clearly stated, the method is not prescribed, and the answer is not known — is the central intellectual skill that CISH develops in its students. Applicants who demonstrate this capacity, whether in their academic record, in their personal statement, or in their interview, distinguish themselves immediately.

**We are looking for intellectual honesty.** We want students who know what they do not understand, who are energised rather than threatened by the encounter with ideas that challenge their existing framework, and who are capable of changing their mind on the basis of evidence and argument. These qualities are harder to demonstrate on paper than examination grades. They are considerably more important.

**We are looking for collaborative instinct.** The intellectual culture at CISH is not competitive in the conventional sense. It is demanding, certainly — but it is demanding in the way that great music-making is demanding: it requires every participant to be excellent and fully committed precisely because everyone's work is in relationship to everyone else's. Applicants who have sought out intellectual community, who have taught or mentored others, who have worked on problems with people rather than in isolation, bring something essential to CISH.

### Undergraduate Admissions

**Entry Requirements**

For UK applicants, CISH typically requires A_A_A at A-level, with specific subject requirements depending on the programme. Mathematics is required for all programmes in the Schools of Mathematical and Computational Sciences and Natural and Engineering Sciences, and is strongly preferred for Political Economy.

International qualifications are considered on their merits. The International Baccalaureate is well regarded; typical offers fall in the range of 42–44 points with 7,7,6 at Higher Level. Other qualifications — the French Baccalauréat, the German Abitur, the Indian Standard XII, the US Advanced Placement system — are assessed with care and experience. We strongly encourage applicants from non-UK systems to contact the Admissions Office to discuss their specific qualifications before applying.

**The Application Process**

_Step One: UCAS Application_ UK and most international undergraduate applicants apply through UCAS by 15 January. The UCAS application includes your academic record, your personal statement, and a reference from your school or college. The personal statement is read with considerable care. It should not be a list of your achievements. It should demonstrate how you think — the questions you find compelling, the investigations you have pursued, the intellectual experiences that have shaped your understanding. The strongest personal statements make the reader want to meet the person who wrote them.

_Step Two: Written Assessment_ Shortlisted applicants are invited to complete the CISH Aptitude Assessment, a two-hour written examination conducted at designated centres worldwide in November. The CAA is not a test of curriculum knowledge. It is a test of reasoning — mathematical, logical, and analytical. There is no syllabus to revise. The most effective preparation is to practise thinking carefully under time pressure on unfamiliar problems. Past papers from 2015 onward are available on the CISH website.

_Step Three: Interview_ Applicants who perform strongly on the CAA are invited to interview at the main campus in December. Interviews at CISH are academic conversations, not assessments of personality or presentation. You will be asked to think through problems you have not encountered before, to defend positions you hold, and to follow an argument wherever it leads — including to conclusions that challenge your initial assumptions. Interviews last approximately 45 minutes and are conducted by two faculty members, typically from the programme to which you have applied and from a cognate discipline.

_Step Four: Offer_ Offers are issued in January following the interview period. Most offers are conditional on A-level or equivalent results. Some exceptional applicants receive unconditional offers following their interview; this is rare, and applicants should not anticipate it.

**Financial Support for Undergraduates**

All home students are eligible for the standard tuition fee loan arrangements. In addition, CISH operates an extensive programme of bursaries and scholarships for students from lower-income households, students from underrepresented geographic or educational backgrounds, and students demonstrating outstanding academic potential without the support structures that more advantaged applicants may have benefited from.

The Crestfield Access Scholarship provides a full fee waiver plus an annual maintenance grant of £8,500 to students from households with incomes below £32,000. Eighteen Access Scholarships are awarded per undergraduate cohort.

The Founders' Fellowship provides a £3,000 annual award to students demonstrating exceptional intellectual achievement, regardless of household income. Forty Founders' Fellowships are awarded per cohort.

We encourage all applicants to contact the Financial Support Office to understand what assistance they may be eligible for before allowing financial considerations to deter an application.

### Graduate Admissions

**Taught Masters**

Applications for taught Masters programmes are submitted directly through the CISH graduate application portal, not through UCAS. The portal opens on 1 October for October entry the following year. Applications are reviewed on a rolling basis, and early application is strongly recommended; several programmes are fully subscribed before the formal deadline.

A strong Masters application typically demonstrates:

- An undergraduate degree of first-class or high upper-second standard from a recognised research university, in a relevant discipline
- A personal statement of no more than 800 words outlining your intellectual interests, your reasons for applying to CISH specifically, and your intentions following the programme
- Two academic references from scholars who can speak to your intellectual capacity and your potential for advanced study
- A writing sample of 2,000–5,000 words demonstrating your capacity for academic argument in your field

Shortlisted applicants for taught Masters programmes are interviewed either in person or by video call, depending on location.

**Doctoral Study**

Doctoral applications require an additional step that distinguishes the CISH DPhil from most other doctoral programmes: the identification of a research question and a proposed supervisor. Before submitting a formal application, prospective doctoral students are expected to develop an initial research proposal — typically two to four pages — and to make contact with a CISH faculty member whose work is relevant to that proposal.

Faculty are not obligated to respond to every approach, but they do respond to approaches that are thoughtful, specific, and demonstrate genuine engagement with their published research. A doctoral application submitted with the informal support of a proposed supervisor is considerably more likely to succeed than one submitted cold. The Graduate Admissions Office can provide guidance on the process of identifying and approaching potential supervisors.

Doctoral funding is awarded on a competitive basis. Full studentships — covering fees at the home rate plus an annual stipend of £19,500, rising annually with inflation — are available for outstanding applicants in most research areas. Several research clusters have additional funding from external grants; the Graduate Admissions Office maintains a current list of funded opportunities.
